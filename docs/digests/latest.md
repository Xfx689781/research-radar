<details><summary>Original abstract</summary>

### [Influence of interindividual variability structures on implied priors and population predictions in pharmacogenetic models](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.27.769637v1)

- **In one sentence**: Re-estimates four variants of a published CYP2D6-dependent codeine population pharmacokinetic model in NONMEM (997 patients), comparing pooled versus phenotype-specific between-person variability structures and how they change simulated morphine formation clearance.
- **In plain words**: People vary in how they process drugs, and models used to predict those differences make assumptions about how much spread to expect in each genetic group. This paper tests how those assumptions, using codeine and its breakdown to morphine in patients with different CYP2D6 gene versions, change the predicted spread of morphine production. - **Background needed**: - Population pharmacokinetics: modelling how a drug's concentrations vary across a group of patients.
- Interindividual variability (IIV): the random differences between people, usually modelled as a spread around a typical value.
- Bayesian prior: the assumed starting distribution before patient data are considered; here IIV largely defines it.
- CYP2D6: a liver enzyme gene with many variants; patients are grouped as poor, intermediate, normal, or ultrarapid metabolizers (activity score is a continuous summary of this).
- Codeine is converted by CYP2D6 into morphine, so enzyme activity drives morphine formation clearance.
- NONMEM and mrgsolve: software for fitting and simulating such models. OFV (objective function value): a fit statistic where a drop indicates better fit.
- Shrinkage: when individual estimates are pulled toward the group average because data per person are limited.
- Coefficient of variation: spread relative to the mean. · level: research-level

<details><summary>Original abstract</summary>

Interindividual variability (IIV) defines part of the prior distribution used in Bayesian parameter estimation. However, the dependence of model-implied priors on IIV and covariate model specifications is rarely examined explicitly. We evaluated empirical support for pooled versus phenotype-specific IIV structures and their consequences for predictive distributions in a CYP2D6-dependent population pharmacokinetic model. Four variants of a published codeine-metabolite model were independently re-estimated in NONMEM using data from 997 patients, combining continuous activity-score or categorical CYP2D6 parameterizations with pooled or phenotype-specific IIV. Distributions of morphine formation clearance were simulated in mrgsolve with 10,000 virtual individuals per phenotype and model. Phenotype-specific IIV improved model fit under both parameterizations ({Delta}OFV -448.57 and -44.27, respectively). The four fitted models differed in the central tendency and dispersion of predicted morphine formation clearance. In controlled simulations, phenotype-specific IIV increased the coefficient of variation of morphine formation clearance in poor metabolizers approximately threefold under the continuous parameterization and by approximately 40% under the categorical parameterization. Both IIV estimates and their effects on population predictions depended on CYP2D6 parameterization. Importantly, improved model fit alone does not establish the biological validity of these distributions, and their implications for individual Bayesian posterior predictions remain to be evaluated.

<details><summary>Original abstract</summary>

Single-cell genomics has been instrumental for studying the variation between cell states. Yet, the use of single-cell technologies to study variation between sample groups remains challenging, with findings often failing to replicate across cohorts. Here, we introduce a general framework for robust single-cell analysis that uses subsampling of individuals to identify statistical patterns that hold regardless of which unmeasured characteristics of those individuals covary with the condition of interest. Upon stimulation, these cells exhibit attenuated proliferation compared with conventional naive T cells and enrichment of regulatory phenotypes. Blocking CTLA4 signaling largely restores the downstream activation program to that of conventional naive cells, suggesting these cells may be a reversible contributor to the age-associated decline in naive T-cell responsiveness. By prioritizing robustness over richer models, our framework enables discovery of previously hidden cellular heterogeneity and offers a general strategy for improving resolution and replicability in single-cell genomics. ### [Argonomics Explorer: an interactive database for dissecting Argonaute-RNA regulatory networks](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.25.769154v1)

- **In one sentence**: Argonomics Explorer is a free, searchable web database that integrates public small-RNA sequencing data from Argonaute-IP and mutant experiments in worm, fly, and human cells to compare AGO-sRNA and AGO-target interactions.
- **In plain words**: Cells use tiny RNA molecules as guides to switch off specific messenger RNAs, and the Argonaute proteins carry those guides. Many research groups have measured which guides sit in which Argonaute proteins, but the data are scattered and hard to compare. This paper presents a free website that gathers these public datasets so users can search by protein, RNA, or guide and compare results across proteins.
- **Why it matters**: The tool makes it easier to see where Argonaute proteins overlap or differ in their roles, which bears on how gene regulation is organized. The authors report a few example findings, such as certain microRNA star strands loading onto AGO-2 but not AGO-1 in flies, and mir-35 family members favoring the worm protein ALG-2. The text does not show how broadly these examples will hold up, so the value of the database for new discoveries is still to be tested by users.
- **Background needed**: - Argonaute (AGO) proteins: the core of RNA-silencing complexes that bind small RNAs.
- Small RNAs (sRNAs): short RNA molecules, such as microRNAs (miRNAs), that guide Argonaute to matching target RNAs.
- AGO-IP: a lab method that pulls out one Argonaute protein with its bound small RNAs to see which ones it carries.
- Star strand: the less-abundant partner strand of a microRNA duplex, usually discarded but sometimes loaded.

- Model organisms: C. elegans (worm), D. melanogaster (fruit fly), and human cell lines. · level: intermediate

<details><summary>Original abstract</summary>

Recent studies in Caenorhabditis elegans, Drosophila melanogaster, and human cell lines have systematically characterized the repertoire of sRNA associations and targets for individual AGOs in these organisms; however, the lack of an accessible, searchable platform limits efficient comparison of the specificity and redundancy of Argonaute functions. Here, we present Argonomics Explorer (AE), an interactive database suite that integrates publicly available sRNA sequencing datasets to enable systematic interrogation of AGO-sRNA and AGO-target interactions across common model systems. AE combines independent AGO-IP datasets, AGO mutant-derived sRNA profiles, and systematic analyses to provide a cross-validated framework for examining AGO-RNA interactions. This database empowers users to query specific AGOs, transcripts, or sRNAs, and to compare associated sRNAs or targets across AGOs. Specifically, we recapitulate findings that certain microRNA (miRNA) star strands are uniquely loaded onto AGO-2 but not AGO-1 in flies. In addition, we report a preferential association of most members of the mir-35 family with the worm Argonaute ALG-2 and the association of the oncogenic oncomiR-1 miRNA superfamily with human AGO2. Such applications illustrate how the AE framework facilitates investigations into AGO specificity, redundancy, and the broader regulatory networks that shape gene expression. The Argonomics Explorer suite is freely available at https://cosbi10.ee.ncku.edu.tw/AE/.

### [Modeling Speech-in-Noise Performance Using an Auditory-Nerve Manifold](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.26.769579v1)

- **In plain words**: People with hearing problems often struggle to follow speech in background noise more than a standard hearing test predicts. This paper uses computer simulations of the hearing nerve to ask whether that gap comes from damaged hair cells in the inner ear or from loss of the nerve fibers that carry sound to the brain. The authors report that simulating nerve-fiber loss across all fiber types matched the accuracy of real listeners closely, while the other scenarios did not.
- **Why it matters**: Standard hearing tests can miss why someone struggles with speech in noise, and separating hair-cell damage from nerve loss could matter for diagnosis and treatment. The text does not show whether the simulated match holds in real patients or in other speech tasks, so its clinical relevance remains to be tested.
- **Background needed**: - Hair cells in the inner ear convert sound into nerve signals; damage to them reduces audibility.

- A neurogram is a picture of nerve activity over time and frequency; an autoencoder is a neural network that compresses such pictures into a small set of numbers.
- Consonant identification is the task of recognizing which consonant was spoken, used here as the speech test.
- Pure-tone average (PTA) is the average hearing threshold across several pitches, in decibels hearing level (dB HL).

### [Concerns over spillover, exclusions, and hospital unit differences in an AI early warning system trial (CONCERN-EWS)](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.23.26368768v1)
*Mann, S.; Berdahl, C. T.; Griffin, B. A.* · `health informatics` · medRxiv doi:10.64898/2026.11.23.26368768 v1 · relevance 0.06

- **In one sentence**: A letter raising methodological concerns (ICU bed spillover, possible exclusion of high-risk patients from the intervention arm, and cluster-level imbalance) about a cluster-randomized trial reporting lower mortality with an AI-based deterioration early warning system.
- **In plain words**: Hospitals are testing AI tools that watch patients' records and flag those who may be getting worse, so staff can step in sooner. In a randomized trial, patients on units using this AI system had about a third lower risk of dying than patients on usual-care units (hazard ratio 0.64). This letter argues that three issues could have made that benefit look bigger than it really is.
- **Why it matters**: Few AI tools have been tested for effects on patient survival in trials, so the design and reliability of such trials matter for how much weight their results deserve. The letter's points are concerns raised by the writers, and the text does not show whether they changed the results.
- **Background needed**: - Cluster-randomized trial: whole hospital units, not individual patients, are randomly assigned to the AI system or usual care.
- Early warning system (EWS): software that flags patients at risk of sudden decline, such as needing ICU care.
- Hazard ratio (HR): a comparison of how quickly an event happens in two groups; 0.64 means about 36% lower rate in the intervention group.
- Spillover: effects of the intervention leaking into the control group, here possibly via competition for scarce ICU beds.
- Exclusion bias: if flagged high-risk patients are moved out of the study group, the group may look healthier than it is.
- Between-cluster variance: differences among units that are not fully accounted for, which can skew results.
- The text is a short commentary; it does not include the original trial's full data or the authors' reply. · level: research-level

<details><summary>Original abstract</summary>

Rossetti et al. report that hospitalized patients whose care was informed by an AI-based clinical deterioration early warning system (EWS) were 36% less likely to die during a pragmatic cluster-randomized trial when compared to patients on usual care units without the EWS (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 0.64; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.53-0.78).1 The authors should be applauded for conducting a trial evaluating the effect of an AI intervention on patient health outcomes; more such trials are needed. Several aspects of the study, however, raise concerns that its findings may not accurately reflect the effect of the EWS. First, spillover effects on availability of scarce intensive care unit (ICU) beds could have led to worse outcomes for patients in the control group. Second, EWS-informed clinical decisions may have led to more frequent exclusion of high-risk patients from the intervention group. Either of these concerns could have led to overestimation of AI benefit. Finally, large pre-trial differences in hospital unit mortality rates suggest that unreported cluster-level imbalance or between-cluster variance could also have affected trial results.
